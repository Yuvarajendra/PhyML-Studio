Pattern analysis

Find the few patterns that describe many blade shapes or noise spectra, and link shape to noise.

Blade shapes: proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)

A blade is described by many numbers (chord, twist and thickness at each radius). POD finds 2–4 combined shape modes that describe almost every blade in a library. The optimiser (Tab 6) can then search over these few mode amplitudes, and every result is still a sensible blade.

Noise spectra: principal component analysis (PCA)

Each test gives a spectrum of 1/3-octave band levels (dB). PCA finds the patterns that separate designs, such as “louder everywhere”, “stronger blade-passing tone” or “more high-frequency hiss”, so you see what kind of noise a change affects.

Shape → noise link: partial least squares (PLS)

PLS regression finds the directions in the blade shape that best predict the measured noise. It shows which radii and which quantities (chord, twist, thickness) drive the noise, with cross-validated accuracy.

POD and PCA are the same eigen-decomposition of a covariance matrix; the names come from fluid mechanics (POD) and statistics (PCA).

The workflow, step by step

1 Geometry. Each propeller is built from a parametric recipe: NACA (National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics) four-digit airfoil sections, a chord distribution, twist set from the pitch-to-diameter ratio (P/D, –) as β(r) = atan(P / 2πr), a thickness ratio t/c (–) from root to tip, and optional sweep, rake, tip rounding, winglets, serrations, porous holes or a ring. Uploaded meshes and computer-aided design (CAD) files are sliced at 18 radii to recover chord, twist and thickness, so they become parametric too. Printability checks flag thin trailing edges, overhangs and an unsafe root.

2 Performance pre-screen. Blade-element momentum theory (BEMT) splits the blade into rings. On each ring the lift and drag of the local airfoil are balanced against the momentum given to the air; Prandtl's tip-loss factor accounts for the tip vortex. Results: thrust T (N), torque Q (N·m), shaft power P = Qω (W), efficiency T/P (g/W), tip Mach number (–) and the blade-passing frequency BPF = B·n/60 (Hz) for B blades at n RPM. Low Reynolds numbers (Re ≈ 20 000–100 000) make the airfoil polar the main source of error; refine it with XFOIL or NeuralFoil.

3 Print and test. Print each design (and ideally two copies), balance it, then run it on the thrust stand through steady throttle steps of 3–5 s. The stand logs voltage (V), current (A), thrust, torque and RPM; a calibrated microphone at a fixed distance records sound pressure (Pa). Record a background (motor-only or silent) file every session.

4 Analysis. Each run is cut into steady steps. Electrical power = V·I (W); efficiency = thrust (gf) / power (W). The microphone signal is turned into a power spectral density with Welch's method, A-weighted (IEC 61672-1) and summed into 1/3-octave bands (IEC 61260-1); the BPF tones are read from the spectrum, and background noise is subtracted energetically. All designs are then interpolated to the same target thrust, because a propeller is always louder when it pushes harder.

5 Pattern analysis. POD/PCA compute the eigenvectors (modes) of the covariance of many “snapshots” (blade shapes or spectra). Leading modes capture most of the variation; each snapshot is summarised by its mode scores. Sparse PCA gives simpler, easier-to-read modes; kernel PCA catches non-linear structure; Hotelling T² and residual Q flag unusual samples; k-means groups similar samples. PLS links shape to noise directly.

6 Optimiser. A Gaussian process (GP) models each measured outcome as a smooth random function with an uncertainty; its kernel (radial basis function, Matérn or rational quadratic) sets how smooth. Automatic relevance determination (ARD) gives each variable its own length-scale. Leave-one-out validation chooses the model. An acquisition rule then picks the next tests: expected improvement (EI), probability of improvement (PI), upper/lower confidence bound (UCB) or Thompson sampling, multiplied by the probability that the efficiency limit holds. For two goals, the expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) enlarges the Pareto front. Batches are built with the “kriging believer” strategy; the first batch uses Latin hypercube sampling (LHS).

7 Learn and repeat. Add the new prints to the test log, reload the data and run Tabs 4–6 again. Typically 3–4 rounds of 3–6 prints show a clear trend.

Common pitfalls

  • Comparing at equal RPM instead of equal thrust. A bigger or steeper propeller is louder at the same RPM simply because it makes more thrust. Always compare at the target thrust.
  • Microphone in the rotor plane or too close. Use a fixed distance (for example 1 m) and angle (for example 30° below the plane), and keep it the same for every test. Never stand in the rotor plane.
  • No background recording. Motor, electronic speed controller (ESC) and room noise can dominate at low thrust. If the measured level is within 3 dB of the background, the result is not reliable.
  • Uncalibrated microphone. Without a 94 dB calibrator tone the absolute dB values are meaningless; differences between designs are still usable only if the gain was never changed.
  • Unbalanced prints. Imbalance adds a strong tone at the rotation frequency (1×RPM) and vibrations that feed the thrust signal. Balance every print.
  • Mixing electrical and shaft efficiency. Electrical efficiency includes motor and ESC losses; shaft efficiency (torque × speed) describes the propeller alone. Choose one in the settings and keep it.
  • Too few repeats. Print-to-print scatter of 0.5–1.5 dB(A) is common. If the differences you look for are of the same size, test two or more copies.
  • Trusting the BEMT absolute values. The pre-screen ranks designs; it is not a prediction of the measured thrust. Use XFOIL polars and calibrate against the first tests.
  • Scaling in POD/PCA. With centring only, the variables with the largest spread dominate the modes (twist in degrees outweighs chord in metres). Use block or standard scaling when mixing quantities.
  • Over-fitting the surrogate. With few designs a flexible model can fit the data perfectly and still predict badly. Trust leave-one-out error, NLPD and coverage, not the training fit.
  • Ranges that are too wide. The optimiser will propose extreme designs at the edge of the ranges. Restrict ranges to what prints well and fits the motor.
  • Changing several things between tests. Motor, battery state, temperature and microphone position all change the result. Log them, and test a reference design (D00) in every session.

Practical test tips

Warm the motor for one minute. Hold each throttle step 3–5 s. Keep the battery voltage within 0.3 V across a session or use a bench power supply. Record at 48 kHz, 24 bit, without automatic gain.

File formats

Blade table CSV: columns r_mm, chord_mm, twist_deg (optional t_over_c, sweep_mm), or normalised r/R, c/R, beta with the diameter given. Rig CSV: columns for time (s), ESC signal (µs), voltage (V), current (A), thrust (kgf or N), torque (N·m) and rotational speed (RPM); rename columns in Tab 3 to match your stand. Audio: mono WAV, any sample rate.
